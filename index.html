Samarthya distributed 300 relief kits comprising Shelter Kits, Hygiene Kits, and Blankets to flood-affected Warkari beneficiaries across multiple locations in Maharashtra. The relief assistance helped vulnerable flood-affected households meet immediate shelter, hygiene, and basic protection needs during the emergency response.

When the rains came

In under six days, 264.8mm of rain fell over Pune district and the Indrayani river flooded. Alandi — where the Sant Dnyaneshwar Maharaj Palkhi begins its 21-day walk to Pandharpur — was cut off, every approach road blocked or submerged, just as lakhs of Warkaris gathered for the 8 July Prasthan.

Most Warkaris are farmers and daily-wage workers carrying only what they need for three weeks on foot. Local administration moved people into 100+ municipal schools, but mats and bedding ran short fast. It was into this that Samarthya's kits, funded by Amazon through DonateKart Foundation, were sent.

What a kit became

A Shelter-cum-Hygiene Kit was never just a box to be opened once. Within hours of handover the same tarpaulin was doing several jobs at once — a dry floor over wet ground, a second roof lashed to a tree or a window ledge, a rain screen for a parked truck, an instant seating mat for a whole Dindi, and, after dark, the blanket that made sleep on the ground possible.

A route-intercept distribution model

Meeting a moving pilgrimage

Warkaris travel in Dindis — self-contained groups of 50–500 led by a Dindi Pramukh. Samarthya reached each one at its own daily halt rather than waiting at a fixed camp.

The Wari runs on paper

Long before Samarthya's truck set out each morning, every Dindi already knew exactly where it would be that day — worked out, printed and carried as a schedule listing each halt, each night's stop and each day's annadata. Reaching a Dindi at the right place depended entirely on reading and trusting this existing system rather than replacing it. These are ten of those schedules, collected in the field.

Eight of the ten name a Dindi or Pramukh that also appears in Samarthya's own distribution records — independent, third-party corroboration of both the Pramukh roster and the route. Scan 1 in particular lists 16 July at Taradgaon and 17 July at Phaltan, matching the Day 2 route exactly.

The Wari on the move

Between the halts there is only the road. These fifteen frames were made along the same corridors the map above plots — Shirur at midday, the Daund–Patas stretch through the afternoon, the Supe crossing at dusk, and the Satara side of the route the following morning. They are what the printed schedules look like when they are actually being walked: a tractor towing a shrine, a numbered placard, a file of white caps thinning out to a knot on an empty carriageway.

Fourteen of these fifteen frames carry GPS coordinates and sit in the same cluster table that draws the map above, which is why each caption can name a time and a place. Two of them also carry their own fixed proof: the arch on the Shirwal–Lonand stretch is an official Government of Maharashtra structure inscribed आषाढी वारी २०२६ and credited to the District Administration, Satara; and the PWD gantry on the Phaltan approach reads Phaltan 12, matching to within a kilometre the GPS distance from that stretch to the Phaltan halt where the Day 2 kits were handed over.

The register, filled in at the halt

Every kit was entered against a name, village, occupation, family size and kit number on the Warkari Flood Emergency Response distribution record — signed or thumb-marked by the beneficiary at the moment of handover.

Serialized token distribution on the move

Before any kit changed hands, each family was issued a serialized paper token carrying its own number, the beneficiary's name and village, and a tick-box for the exact entitlement — one Shelter-cum-Hygiene Kit and one Thermal Blanket. Every token was stamped VERIFIED at issue and marked ISSUED at collection, then matched back against the register, so no family could be counted twice and none could be missed. 234 of the 300 kits were released against a token held directly by the family; the remaining 66 went through the Dindi Pramukh, where rain made one-by-one matching impractical in the queue.

Kits in motion: volunteers and Warkaris carrying the load

Every one of the 300 kits was moved by hand. With no loading bay or trolley at a roadside halt, Samarthya's team and the Warkaris themselves formed the chain together — off the truck bed, down the line, and then on foot into the tented camps or straight up onto the Dindis' own garlanded vehicles.

Community briefings and kit demonstration

At every halt the team addressed the Dindi before distribution began — explaining the process, opening a kit and holding up each item so families knew exactly what they were receiving, and naming Amazon and DonateKart Foundation as the source of the support. The donor banner was displayed at each of these gatherings, and most closed with the Dindi Pramukh's own coconut felicitation.

Kits in hand: the families who received them

Three hundred families walked on with a dry shelter sheet, a hygiene kit and a warm blanket they did not have that morning. These are some of them at the moment of receiving — at the distribution counter, and back at the tarpaulin shelters where the kits were opened and put straight to use.

Human smiles

Between the counting, the queueing and the paperwork, this is what the three days actually felt like — the Wari's own warmth, on faces rather than in numbers. Musicians tuning a tanpura in the shade, water shared hand to hand at a roadside halt, an elder helped up onto a trailer, a woman laughing outright at something just out of frame.
